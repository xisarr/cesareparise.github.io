I am a multisensory perception scientist working across fundamental and applied research. I investigate how the senses interact to generate a coherent representation of our bodies and the environment, and how this information guides action. My research combines psychophysics with ideal observer and stimulus-computable models of multisensory perception and action.

A central strand of this work is the Multisensory Correlation Detector, a biologically inspired model that predicts audiovisual perception directly from sensory input. This research aims to explain the computations linking sensory signals to perceptual experience, behaviour and neural responses.

I apply this understanding to the development of immersive technologies and sensory augmentation. Previously, I worked as a research scientist and principal investigator at Meta Reality Labs, investigating haptics, multisensory integration and spatial audio for virtual and augmented reality. I work with hardware and software engineers on interface design, algorithm development and quantitative perceptual evaluation.

My applied projects include algorithms that localise sound sources in video and generate spatial audio for films with non-spatial soundtracks. I have also developed SonicWhiskers, a sensory substitution system that converts optical input into auditory and haptic feedback. The aim is to help blind and partially sighted people access information about their surroundings and to extend perception when vision is unavailable.

I welcome enquiries about research collaborations and applied projects, including perceptual evaluation, computational modelling and interface development.

Research

My research investigates how the brain processes and combines information from vision, hearing and touch to perceive our bodies and surroundings and guide action. I combine psychophysics, computational modelling and neuroscience to understand the principles of sensory perception, with applications in immersive technologies and sensory augmentation.

Multisensory integration

All animals, even the simplest ones, rely on multiple sensory systems to perceive their bodies and surroundings. My research investigates the computational principles governing multisensory integration, with particular emphasis on vision and audition.

A central question is how the brain determines which sensory signals originate from a common source and should therefore be integrated. I address this question through psychophysics and stimulus-computable models, with applications ranging from sensory substitution to artificial perception and augmented reality.

Spatial hearing

Unlike vision and touch, which rely on spatially organised sensory receptors, the cochlea is organised primarily according to sound frequency. Nevertheless, humans can accurately localise sounds in their surroundings.

My research investigates the computational principles of spatial hearing, including how acoustic cues and natural scene statistics shape sound localisation, how we perceive auditory motion, and how perceptual learning allows us to adapt to unfamiliar acoustic environments.

Touch and motor control

Our hands are our primary means of interacting with the environment. Touch and proprioception provide information about objects and our own movements, contributing to both perception and action.

My research investigates how tactile information contributes to manual interactions and how multisensory integration can compensate for impoverished haptic feedback. A particular focus is the development and evaluation of perceptually informed interfaces for virtual and augmented reality.

From sensory signals to perception

Multisensory Correlation Detector model

A biologically inspired model that turns images and sounds into predictions of what we perceive—and where we look.

One mechanism. Many perceptual effects.

When someone speaks, their movements and voice change together. The Multisensory Correlation Detector (MCD) uses this shared structure to connect information across the senses.

Rather than starting with a description of a stimulus, the model processes the stimulus itself. From pixels and audio samples, it generates predictions about audiovisual timing, cue integration, perceptual illusions and attention. [1]

The gap

What perception achieves is not yet how it works

Multisensory research has been strongly shaped by ideal-observer models, particularly maximum-likelihood estimation and Bayesian causal inference. In David Marr’s terms, these are primarily computational-level accounts: they explain the goals of perception, such as obtaining reliable estimates and deciding which signals belong together.

Their success leaves an important question open. How are these computations performed on the sensory stream? The influential cue-integration models typically begin with quantities such as location, reliability or audiovisual delay. Biological systems must first extract useful information from changing light and sound.

The MCD addresses this algorithmic gap by making the intermediate representations and transformations explicit. It does not reject the achievements of Bayesian models: it asks how behaviour consistent with those accounts can emerge from elementary operations on sensory signals. [1]

Statistical optimality

MLE and Bayesian causal inference explain a lot — but they start later in the computation

Maximum-likelihood estimation (MLE) explains why two sensory cues are often combined in proportion to their reliability. Bayesian causal inference (BCI) explains why that integration weakens or breaks down when the cues are too discrepant, by weighting fusion against the probability that the signals arise from a common cause.

These frameworks are central to modern multisensory research and the MCD is meant to complement them, not replace them. Their main limitation is that they typically operate on experimenter-defined variables — such as cue location, audiovisual delay, cue reliability and their probability distributions. Real perceptual systems do not receive those abstractions. They receive patterns of light and sound, and must infer those quantities from the sensory stream.

The MCD works one step earlier. It operates on signals and their processing: images, sounds, and the changing structure they share over time and space. From a scientific perspective, this means that the problem it solves is closer to the one biological perceptual systems actually face. A further consequence is breadth: the same model that reproduces MLE-like cue combination and BCI-like segregation can also be run on realistic audiovisual scenes, such as a real ventriloquist performance, where classical parametric models do not directly operate. [1]

Statistically optimal models (MLE and BCI)

  • Input: Sensory estimates and their probability distributions, including cue reliability and prior information.
  • Computations: Reliability-weighted cue combination (MLE) and inference about common versus independent sensory causes (BCI).
  • Outputs: Predicted perceptual estimates, biases, precision and patterns of sensory integration or segregation.

Stimulus-computable MCD model

  • Input: Spatiotemporally varying audiovisual signals, including image sequences and acoustic waveforms.
  • Computations: Unimodal transient detection and crossmodal correlation detection, producing dynamic representations of correlation and relative lag of unimodal transients across space and time.
  • Outputs: Correlation and lag responses, spatial correspondence maps and predictions of perceptual judgements, cue integration and attentional orienting.

In the 2025 population-model paper, the spatial population of MCD units reproduces the classic signatures associated with MLE and BCI, while also extending naturally to realistic, dynamic material. This is the bridge between ideal-observer theory and stimulus-computable modelling. [1]

The mechanism

A familiar circuit, a different question

The starting point is the Hassenstein–Reichardt detector, a classic model of motion vision. It compares signals from neighbouring visual locations after delaying one relative to the other. Its component operations have been studied extensively in insect neuroscience, providing a physiologically grounded starting point rather than an arbitrary mathematical construction.

We adapted this principle to compare inputs across senses rather than across neighbouring points in an image. Two complementary outputs indicate how strongly the signals are correlated and which modality leads. Temporal filtering and simple interactions between signals make both outputs evolve continuously over time. [4]

The research programme is a test of sufficiency: how much of multisensory perception can this minimal, biologically plausible mechanism explain? The aim is not a separate model for every illusion, but a shared processing architecture tested against different stimuli, tasks and measures.

The research programme

Evidence, not just a plausible circuit

2012–2013

Correlation matters beyond coincidence

Before developing the model, we tested whether the fine temporal structure of audiovisual signals affects integration. In a localisation task, the gain in precision matched the prediction for optimal integration only when the signals were temporally correlated—even though they occupied the same physical location in both conditions. A subsequent study showed the complementary cost: correlated signals made spatial discrepancies harder to detect. Correlation therefore promotes binding, not simply better performance on every task. [2] [3]

2016

Predicting a percept, not recovering a label

The first MCD was tested with independently generated, random sequences of clicks and flashes. Observers judged whether the streams seemed to share a cause and which seemed to lead. These sequences did not supply a uniquely defined correct classification for the two streams as a whole. Success therefore required matching the stimulus features that shaped human judgements—not merely recovering an experimenter-assigned answer. Given the same sequences, the MCD reproduced the observed classification patterns. [4]

2022

Testing the computations against brain activity

In a magnetoencephalography (MEG) study, participants judged audiovisual sequences while their brain activity was recorded. A time-resolved encoding analysis linked the MCD’s dynamics to temporo-parietal responses. Correlation-related signals were evident across tasks; lag-related evidence was more task-dependent. This connects a model of behavioural judgements to the unfolding neural computations, rather than only to a final response. [6]

2025

What enters the detector? Changes, not sustained intensity

Two psychophysical experiments identified the importance of unsigned transients: changes in a signal, regardless of whether its intensity rises or falls. This matters because a shared event need not make light and sound intensities change in the same direction. The relevant evidence can be that something changes in both senses at the same time.

A striking prediction follows. For periodically modulated stimuli, both onsets and offsets provide matching events. Perceived synchrony can therefore peak twice within one stimulus cycle—when the signals are in phase and when they are half a cycle apart. Observers showed this frequency-doubling effect, and the transient-based MCD reproduced it. [5]

The population model

From clicks and flashes to natural audiovisual scenes

A single detector compares two signals over time. A lattice of detectors extends the same operation across space: each unit compares local visual changes with auditory input. With spatialised sound, the sensory inputs have aligned receptive fields; with a mono soundtrack, the auditory envelope is shared across image locations. The architecture does not require lateral message-passing between detector units; a population normalisation is used for spatial estimation.

This makes the model image- and sound-computable. It can process the footage used in an experiment, rather than being supplied only with its nominal audiovisual delay or spatial discrepancy. The 2025 study tested this architecture across 69 experiments, spanning seven behavioural tasks and data from 534 humans, 110 rats and two monkeys. [1]

Time

Synchrony and temporal-order judgements, temporal adaptation, and the dependence of audiovisual speech illusions on timing.

Space

Reliability-weighted cue combination, the ventriloquist effect, and the integration or segregation of signals described by Bayesian causal inference.

Attention

Audiovisual saliency maps that identify correlated regions of a scene and predict the distribution of gaze during natural viewing.

The important result is not just the number of successful fits. The same low-level computations connect effects usually studied in separate paradigms. MLE-like cue integration and BCI-like segregation emerge from a shared sensory mechanism, rather than from independent explanations for each phenomenon. [1]

What was fitted, and what does the model not claim?

The population study estimated a common set of temporal-filter constants jointly across human timing experiments, with a separate, faster tuning for rats. These constants were then held fixed across additional tests. Depending on the simulation, 0–4 further parameters described task read-out or spatial uncertainty; the gaze analysis required no additional fitted parameters. The model is therefore not wholly parameter-free, and the reported mean correlation is not a held-out accuracy score for every experiment.

The speech simulations explain how audiovisual timing affects integration and the incidence of an illusion, not how a phoneme or word is recognised. The model does not currently include linguistic knowledge or top-down expectations. Its breadth is demonstrated over the tested studies, not over every possible audiovisual phenomenon. [1]

A closer look: the response to natural speech

An unexpected test

Scaling synchrony with distance without explicitly calculating distance

Sound takes longer than light to reach an observer. In a classic experiment, Alais & Carlile (2005) found that subjective audiovisual alignment shifted with perceived source distance, approximately as expected from sound propagation. The result was interpreted in terms of estimating distance and compensating for the associated acoustic travel time. [8]

The MCD reproduces this shift without an explicit distance estimate or a stored speed of sound. The explanation lies in the auditory envelope: in the tested reverberant stimuli, increasing distance weakens the direct onset relative to the reverberant tail. The sound becomes less front-loaded, changing the evidence received by the lag detector.

All four distance conditions were fitted with one shared set of decision parameters, while the temporal filters were held fixed. The separation between the curves therefore arose from the stimulus waveforms, not a different delay correction fitted to each distance. [1]

The broader lesson: structure already present in a sensory signal can support behaviour that appears to require an explicit physical calculation.

The 2025 paper shows these three components together: the distance-dependent click envelopes, the corresponding psychometric curves, and the final scaling of subjective synchrony with distance. This demonstrates a sufficient signal-based explanation of the tested effect; it does not imply that the brain never represents distance. See Figure 2B for the full stimuli and plots.

From a scientific model to applied research

Applied perception projects

A key advantage of stimulus-computable models is that they operate directly on the same audiovisual signals encountered by observers and delivered by technological systems. Unlike conventional models that require predefined descriptions of the stimuli, the MCD can analyse images and sounds themselves, making it possible to investigate how specific properties of real-world stimuli and interface outputs influence perception. This opens new opportunities for applied research, from the perceptual evaluation and optimisation of multisensory interfaces to the development of biologically inspired algorithms for artificial perception.

Perceptual evaluation & immersive interfaces

Use stimulus-specific predictions to investigate audiovisual timing, binding and salience in recordings or prototypes. The model can guide experimental comparisons and help identify which signal features drive a perceptual effect.

Audiovisual localisation & spatial audio

Explore correspondence maps for associating sounds with visible sources. This offers a foundation for bespoke algorithms in audiovisual media, spatial audio and systems that must orient towards relevant events.

Interpretable multimodal AI & robotics

A high response has a concrete explanation: local auditory and visual changes are correlated. Such a module could provide correspondence-based supervision for learning from unlabelled video, or an inspectable sensory front end for a larger system.

The MCD's simple, biologically inspired computations offer a versatile foundation for developing efficient artificial perceptual systems. Its ability to extract meaningful audiovisual correspondences directly from sensory signals opens opportunities for real-time audiovisual processing, multimodal machine learning and robotics, bridging fundamental neuroscience and the development of next-generation perceptual technologies. [1]
